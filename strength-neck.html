Block 1
Neck Reset
5 minutes · 3 exercises · reverse tech neck
Chin Tuck (Cervical Retraction)
2 min · 3 × 10 reps
2 min
Why this matters

Tech neck means your head has drifted forward — each inch adds ~10 lb of load on your cervical spine. The chin tuck re-trains the deep neck flexors that hold your skull directly over your spine, both on the bike and at a desk.

How to do it: Sit or stand tall. Without tilting your chin down, gently glide your head straight back — like you're making a double chin. You should feel a mild stretch at the base of your skull and activation in the front of your neck. Hold 2 seconds, release. Keep your eyes level the whole time.

Common mistake: Tilting the chin downward is not a chin tuck — that works the wrong muscles. The motion is purely horizontal: head travels straight back.

Cervical Side Glide
2 min · 45 sec each side
2 min
Why this matters

Looking down at a screen or down at the road on a TT position creates uneven tension in the scalenes and levator scapulae. This lateral glide decompresses and re-centres the cervical joints.

How to do it: Sit upright. Gently glide your head to the right — move it sideways like a bobblehead, keeping your nose pointing forward (not tilting). Feel a mild stretch on the left side of your neck. Hold 45 seconds. Return to centre, repeat to the left. No rotation, no tilt — purely lateral translation.

If you feel tingling or pins-and-needles in your arms, reduce the range immediately and do gentle side tilts instead.

Levator Scapulae Stretch
1 min · 30 sec each side
1 min
Why this matters

The levator scapulae runs from your shoulder blade to C1–C4 and is chronically tight in cyclists and desk workers alike. Releasing it directly reduces neck pain and improves head-forward posture.

How to do it: Sit in a chair. Anchor your right hand under your seat (to depress the shoulder). Tilt your left ear toward your left shoulder, then rotate your nose 45° downward toward your left armpit. Gently apply overpressure with your left hand on the back of your head. You should feel a pull from the right base of skull down to the right shoulder blade. Hold 30 seconds each side.

Block 2
Cycling Core
10 minutes · 5 exercises · stability over crunches
Dead Bug
3 min · 3 × 8 reps per side
3 min
Why this matters

Cycling demands a stable pelvis while your legs move — the dead bug trains exactly this anti-extension core control. A strong dead bug means more power transfer to the pedals and less lower-back fatigue in long rides.

How to do it: Lie on your back. Press your lower back firmly into the floor — this is the entire foundation. Arms up to the ceiling, knees bent at 90° over your hips. Slowly lower your right arm behind your head and your left leg toward the floor simultaneously. Your lower back must not lift off the floor — if it does, reduce the range. Return to start. Alternate sides. Move at a 3-second count down, 1-second hold, 2-second return.

Too hard? Move only the arm or only the leg, not both at once, until you build the coordination.

Bird Dog
2 min · 2 × 10 reps per side
2 min
Why this matters

Trains the glutes, erector spinae, and deep core together in the exact position your body holds on the bike — hip extended, spine neutral. Critical for preventing the lower-back collapse that shows up after 45+ minutes of hard intervals.

How to do it: Start on hands and knees, wrists under shoulders, knees under hips. Brace your core lightly. Extend your right arm and left leg simultaneously, reaching long rather than high. Hold 3 seconds at the top — your spine should be parallel to the floor, no rotation, no hip hike. Draw your elbow and knee together underneath you (bonus: this adds a crunch), then extend again. Complete 10 per side, then switch.

Side Plank + Hip Lift
2 min · 30 sec each side × 2
2 min
Why this matters

Lateral core stability prevents your hips from rocking side-to-side during hard efforts — a major source of power leakage and knee tracking issues in cyclists. This is also the anti-rotation base that keeps your upper body quiet while your legs hammer.

How to do it: Lie on your side, elbow below shoulder, feet stacked or staggered. Lift your hips so your body forms a straight line ear-to-ankle. Hold 30 seconds. Modified version: Drop your bottom knee to the floor — same benefit, far easier on the shoulder. Progressed version: Add a slow hip abduction (raise your top leg 6 inches) for 10 reps during the hold.

Glute Bridge March
2 min · 3 × 10 reps per side
2 min
Why this matters

Sitting on a spin bike for 45–60 minutes keeps your hip flexors shortened and your glutes inhibited ("gluteal amnesia"). The bridge march reactivates the glutes and reinforces pelvic stability — directly translating to more powerful, efficient pedalling at high cadence.

How to do it: Lie on your back, knees bent, feet flat. Drive hips up into a bridge and hold that height throughout. Slowly lift one knee to 90°, pause 2 seconds, lower it, then lift the other. Your hips must not drop or rotate when you lift — that's the core challenge. If they dip, slow down and reduce the range of the march.

Hollow Body Hold
2 min · 3 × 20 sec holds
2 min
Why this matters

The hollow body is the gold-standard anterior core exercise — it trains the transverse abdominis and internal obliques to maintain a rigid, pressurised torso even when fatigued. This is what stops you from losing power by squirming on the saddle during sprint intervals.

How to do it: Lie on your back. Press your lower back into the floor. Raise your shoulders slightly and lift your legs — start with knees bent and feet at 45° if needed. Arms reach straight overhead (or to the sides for easier version). Hold 20 seconds breathing shallowly. The moment your lower back leaves the floor, stop and rest — this is a no-compromise exercise.

Block 3
Thoracic & Shoulder Reset
5 minutes · 2 exercises · open the chest, free the neck
Wall Angels
2 min · 2 × 10 slow reps
2 min
Why this matters

The handlebar position locks your thoracic spine in flexion and your scapulae in protraction — wall angels actively train serratus anterior, lower trapezius, and thoracic extension. Every rep directly counteracts the rounded-shoulder riding position and gives your neck more room to sit in a neutral position.

How to do it: Stand with your back against a wall, feet ~6 cm from the baseboard. Press your lower back, upper back, and the back of your head against the wall. Place your arms in a "goalpost" position (elbows at shoulder height, backs of hands on the wall if possible). Slowly slide both arms up the wall as high as you can while keeping every contact point on the wall. Slide back down. If your lower back pops off the wall, bend your knees slightly.

Prone Y-T-W Raises
3 min · 2 × 8 reps per shape
3 min
Why this matters

The lower and middle trapezius are chronically inhibited in cyclists and tech-neck sufferers. These fibres are essential for scapular depression and retraction — which directly decompresses the cervical spine. The Y-T-W is one of the most targeted ways to reactivate them with no equipment.

How to do it: Lie face-down on the floor, forehead resting on a towel. Squeeze your shoulder blades lightly before every rep. Y shape: Raise both arms at a 30° angle from your head (thumbs up), hold 2 seconds, lower. T shape: Raise both arms out to the sides at shoulder height, hold 2 seconds, lower. W shape: Elbows bent 90°, raise them toward the ceiling squeezing the blades together, hold 2 seconds. 8 reps each shape = 1 set. These are tiny, effortful movements — 5 cm off the floor is correct.
